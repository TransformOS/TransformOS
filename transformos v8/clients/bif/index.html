Cash position — approximately three weeks of headroom

£360,000 cash at year end against £555,694 of annual interest obligations. On a straight-line basis that is roughly three weeks of cover before the organisation is dependent on contract receipts arriving exactly on schedule.

Compounding it: the property portfolio underpinning £7.1m of bank debt fell in value by £2.825m during the year. Asset cover has weakened at the same time as the cash buffer.

This constraint dictates the sequencing of the entire roadmap. Phase 1 is deliberately weighted toward actions that generate headroom at near-zero investment — property rents to market rate, Focus Futures paid cohorts — rather than the actions with the largest eventual return.

Three weeks is a TransformOS calculation from the filed cash and interest figures, not a figure disclosed in the accounts.

How this score is calculated — and what it is not

What it is. A TransformOS composite scored across the assessed dimensions of the business — commercial, operational, financial, governance, technology and people. Each is rated against what the organisation needs at its current scale, not against a sector average.

What it is not. It is not an audited figure, not a benchmark against a peer set, and not a credit or solvency rating. No external body has verified it. It carries professional judgement and a different assessor could reasonably score it differently.

Why it reads low for a business performing this well. The score weights structural resilience heavily. An organisation can be excellent at everything it does and still score poorly if the model it operates within is fragile. That is precisely the case here — and it is the finding, not a flaw in the scoring.

How to use it. As a baseline to move, not a verdict. Its value is in the re-run: scoring again in twelve months shows whether the transformation is changing the structure or only the revenue line.

The critical constraint is not operational. Delivery is strong, occupancy is 98.29%, and staff culture is exceptional — IIP Double Gold, under 8% turnover. The critical constraint is strategic and commercial: an absence of diversified revenue, inadequate financial infrastructure, and a governance model built for stakeholder management rather than commercial speed.

Business in Focus has 35 years of delivery excellence behind it. The organisation grew 22% last year and operates 14 enterprise hubs across South Wales at near-full occupancy. On every operational measure, it performs.

The exposure sits elsewhere. Over 80% of revenue derives from Welsh and UK Government contract income, creating a structural dependency that represents an existential risk. With approximately £360,000 in cash at year end against £555,694 in annual interest obligations, the organisation has roughly three weeks of headroom. A property portfolio underpinning £7.1m of bank debt fell in value by £2.825m during the year.

The transformation imperative is to monetise what already exists before the next contract cycle forces the conversation on much less favourable terms.

Why £18m might not happen

The trajectory assumes three things that are not yet true.

The commercial capability does not exist. Lever 2 carries £3.5m of the £4.9m uplift, and Defence in Focus alone needs roughly 400 SME sales at a £5,000 average. Business in Focus has not sold a commercial product to an SME in thirty-five years. This is not a resourcing gap that money alone closes — it is a capability the organisation has never built.

The cash position constrains the investment. With approximately three weeks of headroom, the organisation cannot fund a commercial build from operating cash. Every initiative in Phase 1 is therefore chosen for low investment rather than highest return. If the funding source identified in the 90-day action does not materialise, Levers 2 and 3 do not start.

The governance model slows every decision. A fifteen-director stakeholder board with no commercial NEDs is structured for stakeholder representation, not commercial speed. Several initiatives have windows measured in months. Governance reform is sequenced as a Phase 1 prerequisite for exactly this reason.

The four challenges — where this plan could fail

1 · The capability check. Defence in Focus needs ~400 SMEs at £5,000 average over three years. BiF has never sold a commercial product to an SME. The sales capability does not yet exist. This is the single most important execution risk.

2 · The execution difficulty check. The plan assumes the Head of BD manages Northern Valleys bids, the Defence in Focus launch, the first UK ITT and Focus Futures commercialisation simultaneously. That is three people's jobs. Resolution: hire a commercially experienced bid writer and product seller by Month 2, £45–60k.

3 · The focus check. In Phase 1, execute Defence in Focus and AI Adoption only. If resource pressure forces a further cut, Defence in Focus wins every time — the timing is unique and will not recur.

4 · The revenue link check. Focus Diagnostic SaaS is the least certain. Treat as watch-list until the GDPR audit and a commercial conversation with a tech partner are complete. If neither produces evidence of demand by Month 12, remove it from the plan.

Defence in Focus positions Business in Focus as the trusted Welsh SME navigator into the MoD supply chain. It does not compete with commercial consultancies on commercial terms — it wins because it is the organisation Welsh Government backs and Welsh SMEs already trust.

Lead with the trust. Charge for the outcomes.

What would make this NPV wrong

A £387k NPV on a £10–20k investment is a 4.2x return. Returns of that order usually mean the risk sits somewhere other than the capital.

The investment figure excludes the people cost. £10–20k covers product structuring, branding and portal registration. It does not carry the commercial hire the roadmap identifies as necessary at £45–60k per annum. Include that and the return compresses substantially in Years 1 and 2.

Volume is the sensitive variable, not price. The case depends on reaching roughly 400 SMEs over three years. At half that volume the payback moves well beyond Month 17 and the case becomes marginal. Pricing has far less leverage on the outcome than conversion does.

The market timing is an assumption, not a contract. The £50m Wales Defence Growth Deal and the MoD SME spending target create the window. Neither guarantees that Welsh SMEs will pay an intermediary to help them through it. No demand testing has been carried out.

Competitor response is not modelled. If the opportunity is real, defence consultancies and Grant Thornton itself can enter the same space. BiF's advantage is trust and Welsh Government backing, not exclusivity.

The case remains the strongest of the eleven opportunities assessed. But it is a conviction bet on a capability that does not yet exist, in a window that is open now and will close.
